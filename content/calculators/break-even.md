## What is a break-even point?

Your break-even point is the sales volume at which total revenue exactly
equals total costs — no profit, no loss. Sell less than that and you're
losing money; sell more and every extra unit adds to your profit.

It's one of the most practical numbers in small business: before you commit
to a price, a lease, or a hire, break-even tells you exactly how many units
you need to move just to stay afloat.

## Break-even formula

```text
Contribution margin = Price per unit − Variable cost per unit
Break-even units = Fixed costs ÷ Contribution margin
Break-even revenue = Break-even units × Price per unit
```

Fixed costs are the expenses that don't change with how much you sell —
rent, salaries, loan payments. Variable cost per unit is what it costs you
to make or deliver one more unit — materials, packaging, per-unit
commissions. The gap between your price and that variable cost is your
contribution margin: the amount each sale contributes toward covering fixed
costs before any of it becomes profit.

**When variable cost per unit is equal to or greater than price**, there is
no contribution margin to work with — every unit sold loses money or merely
breaks even on its own, so no amount of volume will ever cover fixed costs.
The calculator reports **0 break-even units** in this case instead of
dividing by zero or a negative number. If you see this result, it's a sign
that the pricing itself needs to change, not that you need to sell more.

## Examples

**Example 1 — a small bakery's new product line**

A home bakery has ₱45,000 in fixed monthly costs (kitchen rental, part-time
helper's salary, equipment lease) for a new line of boxed pastries. Each
box sells for ₱150 and costs ₱90 in ingredients and packaging to produce.

Contribution margin = ₱150 − ₱90 = **₱60 per box**.
Break-even units = ₱45,000 ÷ ₱60 = **750 boxes**.
Break-even revenue = 750 × ₱150 = **₱112,500**.

The bakery needs to sell 750 boxes in the month just to cover its costs —
the 751st box onward is where profit starts.

**Example 2 — when the price doesn't cover the cost**

If that same bakery priced its boxes at ₱80 instead of ₱150, while
ingredients and packaging still cost ₱90, the variable cost per unit
(₱90) would exceed the price (₱80). The contribution margin would be
negative, so the calculator returns **0 break-even units** — there's no
sales volume that can rescue a price set below its own cost to produce.
The fix is to raise the price or cut the variable cost, not to sell harder.

## Break-even and your fixed costs

Break-even is especially sensitive to fixed costs, since every peso of rent
or salary has to be earned back through contribution margin before you see
a profit. A business with high fixed costs needs either a higher
contribution margin per unit or much more volume to reach the same
break-even point as a leaner operation. This is also why break-even is the
natural companion to the [profit margin calculator](/profit-margin-calculator)
— margin tells you whether each individual sale is healthy, while
break-even tells you how many of those sales you actually need.
