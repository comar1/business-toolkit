## What is markup?

Markup is the percentage you add on top of your cost to arrive at a selling
price. It answers a pricing question that's the mirror image of margin: if
this is what the item cost me, how much am I adding to make a profit?

Markup is the natural starting point when you're pricing a product — you
know your cost first, then decide how much to add on top before you ever
see what the final revenue or margin looks like.

## Markup formula

```text
Profit = Selling price − Cost
Markup (%) = (Profit ÷ Cost) × 100
```

If cost is ₱0, markup is shown as 0%, since dividing by zero isn't
meaningful. Cost is what you paid to acquire or produce the item. Selling
price is what you charged for it. The result is a percentage: how much
profit you earned relative to what you spent.

## Examples

**Example 1 — a reseller pricing a product**

An online reseller buys a gadget for ₱200 (cost) and lists it for ₱350
(selling price). Profit is ₱350 − ₱200 = ₱150, and the markup is (₱150 ÷
₱200) × 100 = **75%**.

**Example 2 — a small eatery pricing a meal**

A carinderia's cost to prepare a meal is ₱60 (cost), and it sells for ₱85
(selling price). Profit is ₱85 − ₱60 = ₱25, giving a markup of (₱25 ÷ ₱60)
× 100 = **41.7%** — a useful check on whether that price covers enough
above ingredient cost to be worthwhile.

## Markup vs margin

Markup and margin both describe profit, but they use different
denominators: markup is profit over **cost**, margin is profit over
**selling price**. For the same sale, markup will always be the larger
number whenever there's a profit, because cost is smaller than the
resulting price. If you're starting from a known cost and deciding how
much to charge, markup is usually the more natural number to work with;
once you have a final price, the [profit margin calculator](/profit-margin-calculator)
shows how that price performs as a share of revenue.
