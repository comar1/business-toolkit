## What is an invoice total?

An invoice total is the full amount a client needs to pay you, including any
tax added on top of your price. Most invoices show three numbers: the
**subtotal** (your price before tax), the **tax** charged on that price, and
the **total** the client actually owes.

Getting this right matters for two reasons: your client needs a clear,
correct breakdown to pay and record the expense, and you need to know how
much of what lands in your account is yours to keep versus tax you're
collecting on behalf of the government.

## Invoice calculator formula

```text
Tax amount = Subtotal × (Tax rate ÷ 100)
Invoice total = Subtotal + Tax amount
```

Subtotal is the price of your goods or services before tax. Tax rate is the
percentage charged on top of that price. The calculator multiplies the two
to get the tax amount, then adds it back to the subtotal to get the final
invoice total — the number your client actually pays.

## Examples

**Example 1 — a freelance design invoice**

A freelance graphic designer bills a client ₱15,000 for a logo package and
charges a 12% tax rate (used here as an illustrative example rate — confirm
your own applicable rate separately). Tax amount is ₱15,000 × (12 ÷ 100) =
**₱1,800**. Invoice total is ₱15,000 + ₱1,800 = **₱16,800**.

**Example 2 — a small catering order**

A home-based catering business invoices a client ₱42,500 for a weekend event
order, again using a 12% tax rate as an example. Tax amount is ₱42,500 ×
(12 ÷ 100) = **₱5,100**. Invoice total is ₱42,500 + ₱5,100 = **₱47,600** —
the amount that goes on the final invoice sent to the client.

## Invoice total vs tax calculator

This calculator and the tax calculator solve opposite problems. Here, you
start with a subtotal and add tax on top to arrive at a total — the usual
flow when you're preparing an invoice to send out. The
[tax calculator](/tax-calculator) does the reverse: it starts from an
existing total and splits out how much of that figure was tax, which is
useful when you're reviewing a bill or receipt you've already received.
Actual tax treatment depends on your business type, registration, and local
regulations, so treat both tools as estimators rather than filing guidance.
