## What is cash flow?

Cash flow tracks the actual money moving in and out of your business over a
period — not what you've earned on paper, but what's really in your bank
account. It answers a practical question: did your cash position get
stronger or weaker, and how much do you have left to work with?

Cash flow matters because bills, payroll, and suppliers don't accept
invoices as payment — they need actual cash, when it's due. Tracking it
regularly helps you catch a cash squeeze while you still have time to act.

## Cash flow formula

```text
Net cash flow = Cash in − Cash out
Closing balance = Opening balance + Net cash flow
```

Cash in is all the money that actually came into your account during the
period. Cash out is all the money that actually left it. Opening balance is
what you had on hand at the start of the period. Closing balance is what
you're left with at the end.

## Examples

**Example 1 — a healthy month**

A small retail shop starts the month with an opening balance of ₱45,000.
Over the month, ₱180,000 in cash in comes from customer payments, and
₱130,000 in cash out covers inventory, rent, and staff wages. Net cash flow
is ₱180,000 − ₱130,000 = ₱50,000, and the closing balance is ₱45,000 +
₱50,000 = **₱95,000**. The shop is building up cash reserves.

**Example 2 — a tight month with slow-paying clients**

A small contracting business starts the month with an opening balance of
₱30,000. Several clients delay payment, so only ₱95,000 in cash in comes
through, while ₱120,000 in cash out still goes to materials, subcontractors,
and payroll. Net cash flow is ₱95,000 − ₱120,000 = **−₱25,000**, and the
closing balance is ₱30,000 − ₱25,000 = **₱5,000**. The business is still
holding a small positive balance, but the shortfall is a warning sign to
follow up on unpaid invoices before the next payroll run.

## Cash flow vs profit

Cash flow and profit measure different things, and it's common for them to
disagree in the same period. Profit includes sales you've invoiced even if
the customer hasn't paid yet, and it spreads some costs (like equipment)
over time rather than counting them all at once. Cash flow only counts
money that has actually moved.

That's why a business can be profitable on paper but still run out of cash —
for example, if a big batch of invoices goes unpaid for 60 days while rent
and payroll keep coming due every month. The reverse can also happen: a
business can show weak or negative profit in a given month while cash flow
stays positive, if it collected on old invoices or took out a loan. Neither
number replaces the other — profit tells you if the business model works,
cash flow tells you if you can pay your bills this month.
